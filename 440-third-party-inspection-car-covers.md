# Third-Party Inspection for Car Covers: What Inspectors Miss

A third-party inspection is worth the fee, and it is also routinely misread as a guarantee of quality. An inspector with a checklist will find a crooked seam, a wrong label or a short carton count. It will not tell you whether the cover fits, whether the print survives being stretched, or whether the fabric will chalk within a season.

Those gaps are predictable, and they can be closed by writing a better brief.

## What a standard inspection does cover

A general AQL inspection samples cartons against a checklist:

- workmanship — stitching, seam alignment, loose threads, hem finish
- dimensions against the approved specification
- colour against an approved swatch
- labelling, polybag and carton content
- packing count and carton marking

This catches the defects that are visible in a warehouse, on a flat table, in daylight. That is a narrower set than most buyers assume.

## Where it stops

**Fit.** An inspector measures the cover flat. A fitted cover is judged on a vehicle, and the failures — a mirror pocket that pulls, a hem that lifts at the bumper, a cover that will not sit over a roof aerial — only appear when it is on the car.

**Stretch and recovery.** On a stretch cover, the property that matters is whether it returns after being pulled. Measuring a relaxed panel tells you nothing about that.

**Print under tension.** A printed area can pass inspection flat and craze the first time it is stretched over a body panel.

**Odour.** Coated fabrics can carry a solvent or plasticiser smell that only becomes obvious after a carton has been closed for weeks. Inspectors rarely record it unless asked.

**Longevity.** Colour fastness and UV performance cannot be seen in a warehouse. They need a test report, not an inspection.

## How to extend the brief

Write these into the inspection scope, and accept that they cost more:

- fit the sample on a real vehicle and photograph the stress points
- pull a stretch cover to its stated elongation and check recovery
- stretch the printed area and inspect for edge lift
- open cartons that have been sealed for at least several days and record odour
- retain a production sample and keep it for the length of the warranty

The last one matters most in a dispute. An inspector's photographs prove what was seen; a retained sample proves what was shipped.

## Choosing and briefing the inspector

Ask for the report format before booking, and check that it records measurements as numbers rather than "pass". Ask how discrepancies are handled — who is called, and whether inspection stops or continues.

Give the inspector your specification, not the factory's. If the only document available is the supplier's own spec sheet, the inspection validates the supplier against itself.

## The limit worth accepting

An inspection reduces the chance of a bad shipment; it does not transfer responsibility. The factory still owes you a conforming product, and the inspection clause should say that plainly rather than implying the report ends the conversation.
