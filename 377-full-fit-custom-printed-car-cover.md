# Custom Full-Fit Printed Car Covers: An OEM Buyer's Guide to Patterns, Printing & Production

"Full-fit" car covers are a different product category from the covers you find in a supermarket aisle. Instead of a loose universal shell, a full-fit cover follows the exact exterior contours of one specific vehicle — body panels, mirrors, windows, light clusters — and is frequently custom-printed with the buyer's own graphics, colors and logo. Converters, dealerships, accessory brands and even car manufacturers use them for indoor display, showroom protection and transport, often as branded merchandise for their own vehicles.

If you are planning to launch such a product under your own brand, here is what you need to understand before choosing a manufacturing partner.

## 1. Patterns come from vehicle data — not from a magic database

A contour-accurate cover can only be as good as the pattern it is cut from. The key question to ask any factory is: *where does your pattern come from?* There are three honest answers:

- **From your data** — you provide the vehicle's blueprint, a 2D pattern file (AI or DXF), or 3D data (OBJ).
- **From their own scans or measurements** — only reliable if the factory actually measured the specific vehicle; patterns that are "close to" the model are not good enough for full-fit.
- **From a shared "database"** — be careful. Databases with unknown vehicle generations and years are exactly how full-fit covers end up baggy on the A-pillar and tight over the mirrors.

Serious OEM partners will not guess. They take your make, model and year, combine it with your artwork or 3D file, and run it through their own 2D/3D design team to build a production pattern that matches the actual contours. If a factory claims they "have every pattern already" without asking for your vehicle data, treat that as a red flag.

## 2. Indoor full-fit covers have their own material logic

Most premium full-fit covers are **indoor products** — for garages, showrooms, dealerships and enclosed transport. That changes the material conversation completely:

- The classic fabric is **230 GSM spandex with fleece backing**. Spandex gives the stretch that lets the cover hug compound curves and mirror housings; the fleece back protects the paint; 230 GSM is the sweet spot between too-light and too-heavy.
- The material is **breathable and paint-safe** — exactly what an indoor cover should be.
- It is **not waterproof and not UV-rated**. If a supplier markets this fabric as an outdoor weather cover, the product will fail in rain and sun. Know your positioning: indoor premium protection, not outdoor weather defense.

## 3. Printing and branding decide whether it is "your" product

A full-fit cover becomes a branded product through printing and finishing. Confirm the factory supports:

- Full-surface custom printing (your graphics and colors), not just a small logo patch
- Custom stitching, labels and hang tags
- Packaging choices — typically color boxes, drawstring bags or duffel bags
- Entire production under your brand, from fabric to outer carton

Also agree on file formats up front: **AI or DXF for 2D artwork, OBJ for 3D vehicle data** is a practical, industry-standard setup.

## 4. Sampling economics: prototype first, always

A full-fit cover is not the product to bulk-order blind. The standard professional sequence:

1. One prototype/sample with your design, quoted at a fixed sample cost (a typical starting point is around USD 350 for a 230 GSM sedan-size indoor cover — exact cost depends on vehicle size and design complexity).
2. Sample lead time of roughly **7–10 working days** once the artwork is confirmed usable.
3. Fit review, then revisions. Agree the revision economics in writing: design-driven revision rounds are usually charged at the sample rate, while genuine sewing or manufacturing defects should be corrected at the factory's cost.

## 5. MOQ and lead times for a young brand

If you are testing a market before committing volume, look for a partner who lets you start small:

- **MOQ of 1** — order a single sample first, then a small test batch.
- **Mixed models in one order** — combine several vehicle models in your first batch instead of meeting a per-model minimum.
- **Production lead time around 2–4 weeks** after sample approval is realistic for this product type.

## 6. Quality control you can verify

Ask exactly what checks happen before shipment. A trustworthy process looks like this: grey fabric inspected before printing → printed fabric inspected after printing → 100% inspection of finished goods after sewing → random inspection before delivery. And ask about the defect policy: a professional factory will remake any item that does not match the approved sample, and should state its claim window in writing (a common standard is 7 days from receipt for visible sewing/printing issues).

## The bottom line

Custom full-fit printed car covers are an OEM product where honesty about patterns and materials matters more than marketing. Work with a factory that asks for your vehicle data, tells you clearly that the product is indoor-rated, samples before bulk, and lets you start with one piece — not one container.
