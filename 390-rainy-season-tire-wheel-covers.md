# Rainy Season Tire and Wheel Covers

In a wet season, the most exposed parts of a parked car are not the panels — they are the tyres and wheels. Water pools in the tread, holds against the wheel face, and sits in the gap between the tyre and the ground. Over weeks of rain, that is where the visible deterioration appears first.

Whether a cover is the right answer depends on which of two different problems is being solved.

## Two different products, two different jobs

**Tyres and wheels fitted to a parked vehicle.** Here the goal is to keep standing water off the wheel face and reduce the wet-dry cycling that dulls finishes and rusts brake components. This is a parking problem.

**Tyres in storage.** A second set taken off for the season is stored flat or stacked, and the threats are different: ozone, sunlight, heat, and being deformed by its own weight or by stacked load. This is a storage problem, and it calls for a storage cover rather than a wheel cover.

Conflating the two is the most common mistake in this category.

## Fitted wheels in a wet season

For a car parked through a rainy season, the relevant issues are:

- **Water pooling in the tread** keeps the rubber wet continuously, which encourages the surface cracking that shows up later.
- **Brake discs and calipers** corrode quickly when wet and unused. A car parked for weeks in the rain develops a rust film that takes a few stops to clear, and left long enough it becomes pitting.
- **Wheel finishes** — polished, diamond-cut and painted faces — mark more readily when they are repeatedly wetted and dried.

A wheel cover addresses the first and last of these by keeping rain off the outer face. It does not stop moisture from the ground or from the air, so it should not be sold as full protection.

For a vehicle parked long-term in a wet season, the bigger wins are simpler: park on a hard, draining surface rather than grass or gravel, and move the car periodically so the discs dry and the contact patch changes.

## Tyres in storage

For a stored set, the priorities are well established:

- **Block light.** UV and sunlight degrade rubber.
- **Keep them cool.** Heat accelerates ageing whether or not the tyres are in use.
- **Reduce ozone contact.** Ozone attacks rubber and is present around electrical equipment, so a workshop is a poor storage location.
- **Avoid deformation.** Rotate stacked tyres periodically, or store them on rims with the load spread.
- **Keep them dry and ventilated.** A sealed bag that traps moisture is worse than a breathable cover.

A storage cover for this purpose should be opaque, breathable and sized to the stack.

## The wet-season routine

1. Wash the wheels before covering, and let them dry. Covering a wet wheel traps the water the cover was meant to exclude.
2. Fit wheel covers securely. A loose cover flapping against a wheel face at the mercy of the wind is an abrasive.
3. Move or run the car periodically through a long wet season, so the discs and the contact patch do not sit in one position for months.
4. Rinse the covers when the season ends, dry them fully, and store them dry.

## Buying notes

- fitted wheel covers for parking, breathable stack covers for storage
- opaque material for stored tyres, to block light
- secure attachment for anything fitted to a parked car
- a material that tolerates being wet for weeks without degrading
