# Pet Cover for Hatchbacks

A hatchback changes the pet cover problem through the vehicle rather than through the dog. The rear bench is short, the roof is low, the seatbacks fold to create a cargo floor immediately behind the passenger compartment, and the boot opening is the same opening the dog may use to board.

Each of those facts removes an option that works in a larger car.

## The hatchback constraints

**A short rear bench.** The cushion depth is less than in a saloon or SUV, so a cover cut for a standard rear bench overhangs the front edge, folds under the knees and blocks the belts. Depth matters more than width here.

**A low roof.** A hammock suspends from the front and rear headrests. In a hatchback, the rear headrests are close to the tailgate glass and the roofline is low, so a deep hammock crowds the dog and can press against the rear screen.

**Fold-flat seats.** In most hatchbacks the rear seat folds to form a load floor. A hammock or a full bench cover gets in the way of that, and the owner folds the seat far more often than a saloon owner does.


## Which arrangement suits a hatchback

**A boot-liner style cover used with the seats folded** is often the best answer for a hatchback, particularly for medium and large dogs. It uses the folded seatback as the floor and protects the whole cargo area rather than trying to cover a short, awkward bench.

**A flat bench cover with door flaps** suits small dogs that ride on the rear seat and stay there. It keeps the seatbacks clear so the fold still works.

**A shallow hammock** can work, but only if the cover leaves the rear screen unobstructed and there is genuine headroom for the dog to stand and turn.

The worst choice in a hatchback is a deep hammock sized for an SUV: it takes the headroom, obstructs the screen and cannot be left in place when the seats are folded.

## Measuring a hatchback rear bench

1. **Cushion depth** from the seatback joint to the front edge, which is the dimension most often overestimated.
2. **Width** at the widest point, and at the door apertures.
3. **Seatback height**, and whether the headrests are removable or fixed.
4. **The fold line** on the seatback, if the cover must stay in place while folding.

## Practical setup notes

- Check the cover does not foul the parcel shelf or cargo cover, which sit close to the seatback and will not retract over a raised cover.
- If the dog boards through the tailgate, the boot lip and bumper take the wear. A boot-liner arrangement handles that; a seat cover does not.
- Watch the rear belts, whose buckles sit close to the cushion edge on a short bench.

## For small dogs specifically

For a small dog on the rear seat, the hatchback is a good platform: a flat cover plus door flaps covers everything within reach, the short bench allows an accurate fit, and the cover folds away with the seat. The requirement is a cover cut to the smaller depth rather than a universal size. The typical failure is an overhanging front edge that folds under the knees and hides the belts.

## Buying notes

- depth measured to the vehicle, not a universal rear-bench size
- a flat cover plus door flaps, or a boot-liner arrangement
- a design that does not block the fold-flat mechanism
- no obstruction of the rear screen or parcel shelf
- unobstructed seatbelt buckles
