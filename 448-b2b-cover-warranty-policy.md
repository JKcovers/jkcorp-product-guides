# Writing a B2B Warranty Policy for Covers

A consumer warranty is a marketing document. A B2B warranty is a contract clause, and it needs to be written so that both parties know what happens when a container arrives wrong — because that is the only moment it will be read.

Most cover warranties fail for the same reason: they promise a duration without defining a defect.

## Define the defect, not the duration

"Two-year warranty" means nothing until it says what is covered. A workable definition separates three categories:

**Manufacturing defect.** A seam that opens, a zip that fails, a hem that is not attached, a panel cut to the wrong dimension. These are the supplier's responsibility and should be covered for a stated period from delivery.

**Material failure.** Coating delamination, fabric tearing at normal use, colour loss beyond an agreed threshold. These sit between supplier and buyer, because they depend on the material specification that was agreed. If the specification did not state a standard, this category becomes an argument.

**Wear and misuse.** UV chalking after years outdoors, abrasion from a rough surface, damage from a hot exhaust, tearing from wind. These should be excluded explicitly, because they are the majority of real claims.

## Duration that matches the product

A single duration across a range is usually wrong. An indoor dust cover and an outdoor all-season cover do not have the same exposure, and warranting them identically either overpromises on one or underprices the other.

Set the period by product class and by the exposure it is designed for, and say what the period is measured from — shipment date, arrival date, or date of sale to the end user. The three are different by weeks, and the difference matters when a claim lands at the boundary.

## The claim process

A warranty without a process becomes a negotiation. Specify:

- how a claim is raised, and to whom
- what evidence is required — photographs, batch code, carton label, order reference
- the response time for an initial assessment
- who pays return freight on a confirmed defect
- the remedy hierarchy: replacement, credit, or repair

The remedy hierarchy is the clause that prevents most disputes. If replacement is the default and credit is the fallback, both sides know the outcome before the argument starts.

## Exclusions worth stating

- damage caused by incorrect installation or use
- products altered after delivery
- claims raised after the period, without documentation
- normal colour variation between dye lots
- damage in transit where the buyer arranged freight

That last one is frequently contested. If the buyer's forwarder collected the goods, transit damage is a freight claim, not a warranty claim — and saying so in advance avoids an unwinnable argument.

## Making it enforceable

A warranty is only as good as the records behind it. Batch codes on cartons, retained production samples, and a written specification give a claim something to be measured against. Without them, every dispute becomes one party's word against the other's.
