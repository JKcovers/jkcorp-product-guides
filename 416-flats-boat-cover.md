# Flats Boat Cover Guide

A flats boat is built low and light for shallow water, and that geometry makes it awkward to cover: little height, a poling platform above the transom, and a deck close to the waterline.

A bay-boat cover sits too high and too loose here; a correct cover is cut for a very different profile.

## The defining features

- **Very low freeboard**, so the cover's hem sits close to the water.
- **A flat, wide casting deck** forward, often with a large open area.
- **A poling platform** above the outboard, which is a hard point and a projection.
- **Low or no windshield**, with a small console.
- **Shallow draft**, so the hull sits high in the water but the deck sits low.

## Water and the low deck

The central problem is that a flats boat's deck is nearly at the waterline, and the cover follows it.

- **Splash from below** wets the underside of the cover more than rain wets the top.
- **A low hem** can sit in the water on a moored boat, which wicks moisture up into the fabric.
- **A flat cover** holds water across its whole surface rather than shedding it.

The answer is a cover that is crowned, even slightly, and a hem that sits above the waterline with a positive closure. On a moored flats boat, this often means the cover must be pulled up rather than allowed to hang.

## The poling platform

The platform is the feature most often missed in a generic pattern.

- **It projects above and behind the transom**, so a cover that ends at the transom will not clear it.
- **It is a hard, often aluminium structure**, so contact points need reinforcement.
- **It is a load path in wind**, and a cover attached to it must account for the leverage.

Options are to cover the platform with the boat, to cut the cover around it, or to remove it. For a boat kept rigged, covering it is usual, and the pattern must be built for that height.

## Trailering and the light hull

Flats boats are commonly trailered long distances to fishing grounds, and the light hull matters.

- **The cover must close at the hem**, because an unsupported span on a light boat flaps violently at speed.
- **The hull's low profile means the cover is fully exposed** to airflow, with no cabin or top to shield it.
- **Straps must not pull the hull** or load the gunwale, which on a light boat can flex.

## Support

With little height available, support has to be low-profile:

- **A short pole over the console**, lifting the cover just enough to shed water sideways.
- **The console itself**, which is usually the highest hard point forward of the platform.
- **A built-in crown** in the pattern, which suits a narrow boat and avoids hardware.

A tall pole is counterproductive here: it creates a sail on a boat that is already light and exposed.

## What to look for

- a pattern cut for low freeboard, with the hem above the waterline
- clearance and reinforcement at the poling platform
- a closing hem suitable for trailering
- a slight crown or a short console pole for drainage
- sealed seams, because splash from below is the dominant water path
- vents, since a sealed low cover condenses heavily

## Buying notes

Ask whether the pattern includes the poling platform, and at what height. This is the measurement that separates a flats boat cover from a generic low-profile cover, and it is the one most often omitted from a buyer's brief.
