# Autumn Dew and Condensation Covers

Autumn produces the largest daily temperature swings of the year in most temperate regions: warm afternoons, clear nights, and a rapid drop after sunset. That pattern is the ideal recipe for dew, and dew is the mechanism behind most of the surface damage that appears during the season.

A cover helps, but only if it lets the moisture it collects escape.

## Where autumn dew comes from

During the day the car's panels absorb heat. After sunset the surfaces radiate that heat to a clear sky and cool faster than the surrounding air. Once a surface drops below the dew point of the air around it, moisture in that air condenses directly onto it.

The result is a car that is soaked every morning without a drop of rain. Three details follow:

- **Clear, calm nights produce the heaviest dew.** Cloud cover and wind both suppress it.
- **Horizontal surfaces dew first and heaviest** — roof, bonnet, boot, and the tops of the mirrors.
- **Dew forms on the cover too.** A cover exposed to the same sky cools the same way, so the top of the cover is wet every morning.

That last point is where covers are judged. The question is not whether the cover gets wet; it is what happens to that moisture next.

## The single specification that matters: breathability

Dew on the outside of a cover is unavoidable. Moisture becoming trapped on the inside is avoidable, and it is the failure mode that damages paint.

A cover that blocks water absolutely — a solid, non-breathable film — will also block the escape of moisture that has condensed underneath it. The result is a covered car that stays damp for hours after an uncovered one has dried, and persistent damp under a cover is what produces water spotting, mould growth in seams and trim, and a musty interior.

The practical specification is therefore a layered or coated fabric that resists liquid water from outside while allowing water vapour to pass from inside. Coated-woven fabrics and multi-layer laminates do this. A plain waterproof film does not.

## Autumn routine

1. **Do not store a cover wet.** Folding a damp cover into a bag creates a mildew culture. Dry it fully, or hang it, before storage.
2. **Remove the cover in the morning if the forecast is dry.** An uncovered car dries far faster than a covered one.
3. **Rinse the cover weekly.** Autumn brings leaves, sap and grit alongside the moisture, and all three hold water against the fabric.
4. **Clear the drainage channels.** The scuttle, door drains and sunroof channels collect leaf debris in this season. Blocked drains put water where the cover cannot help.
5. **Watch the underside of the cover.** A cover that is dry on top and damp underneath is the signal that breathability is inadequate for the conditions.

## Cover choice for the season

- a coated or laminated fabric, not a solid waterproof film
- correct fit, because a loose cover traps a larger volume of moist air
- light colour, which cools less overnight and therefore collects less dew
- a surface that sheds leaf debris rather than holding it

## The wider point

Autumn damage is usually attributed to rain. In practice, most of it is done by dew: repeated nightly wetting with no wind to dry the surface, followed by a season in which the temperature keeps crossing the freezing point. Whatever cover is used, the goal is the same — keep liquid water off the paint while letting vapour escape.
