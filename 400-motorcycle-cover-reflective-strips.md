# Specifying Reflective Strips on Motorcycle Covers

Reflective strips are the cheapest safety feature on a motorcycle cover and the one most often specified badly. A strip that is the wrong material, in the wrong place, or attached the wrong way provides no benefit and adds cost without reducing risk.

The practical questions are what material, where on the pattern, and how it is fixed.

## Two materials that are not the same

**Retroreflective material** returns light back towards its source. It appears bright only when illuminated by a light — a headlamp, a torch — and is invisible in the dark otherwise. This is what makes a covered bike visible to an approaching driver.

**Fluorescent material** is bright under ambient light and appears vivid in daylight or dusk. It does not reflect, so it does nothing in the dark.

These are often confused. A cover described as "reflective" that uses fluorescent fabric will look impressive in a photograph taken with flash and will be useless on an unlit street. A specification should name the material behaviour, not the colour.

Many combine the two: a fluorescent background with retroreflective strips over it, working in both daylight and darkness.

## Where the strips belong

Placement should follow the sightlines of an approaching driver, not the aesthetics of the pattern.

- **Across the rear**, at the height of a following vehicle's headlamps. This is the most important strip on the cover, because rear-end collisions into parked bikes are the common scenario.
- **Along the sides**, roughly at the same height, so the bike is visible from an oblique angle.
- **On the upper front**, where a driver approaching from ahead will see it. On a motorcycle cover this often means the bar or screen area.
- **Not on the top**, where a strip is rarely in a headlamp beam.

The general principle: put the strip where a light will hit it, at the height a driver's lamps actually reach.

## Attachment methods

**Stitched-on tape.** The most durable option. The stitching line is the failure point, so the tape must be wide enough to carry a seam without delaminating.

**Heat-applied transfer.** Smooth and unobtrusive, and it does not create stitch holes in a waterproof panel. Its weakness is that it can lift at the edges after repeated folding or washing.

**Screen-printed reflective ink.** Cheapest and least durable. Suitable for a small logo rather than a functional strip.

**Sewn-in panels.** A whole panel of reflective fabric rather than a strip. Durable, but adds cost and can stiffen the cover.

For a cover that is folded and unfolded daily, stitched tape on a reinforced backing is the most reliable combination.

## What degrades performance

- **Dirt and road film.** Retroreflection falls off as the surface becomes soiled, which on a motorcycle cover happens quickly.
- **Abrasion at fold lines.** A strip placed exactly where the cover creases will crack first.
- **Washing.** Harsh detergents and hot water attack the reflective layer, and the loss is invisible until the cover is next lit up.
- **UV exposure over time.** All retroreflective materials degrade, and the rate depends on the product.

This is why placement should avoid fold lines and why the care label matters: a reflective strip that is washed aggressively stops working while still looking intact.

## What to ask a supplier

- the material type, described by behaviour rather than colour
- the width and placement on the pattern, marked on a drawing
- the attachment method
- whether the material has a stated retroreflective performance and how it was measured
- care instructions specific to the strip
