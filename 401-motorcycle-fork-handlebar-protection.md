# Motorcycle Fork and Handlebar Protection

The fork tubes and the handlebar fittings are the parts of a motorcycle that a conventional cover protects least well, and they are among the most expensive to replace. A cover that reaches the ground at the rear often stops short at the front, where the stanchions sit.

What damages these parts explains why the lower front of a cover deserves attention.

## What damages fork tubes

**Stone chips.** The exposed stanchion above the lower fork leg is in the direct line of fire from the front wheel. A chip through the hard chrome creates a raised edge that then passes through the fork seal on every stroke.

**Seal failure follows pitting.** Once damaged, oil escapes and dirt enters. This is the mechanism behind most fork seal replacements, and it begins with a small impact.

**Corrosion under road film.** Salt and road film held against the tube by a cover's hem or by a strap can pit the surface in the same way.

**UV and ozone on the dust wiper.** The rubber wiper at the top of the lower leg degrades with exposure, and a perished wiper lets grit past.

The cover's contribution is not armour but cleanliness: keeping the tube dry and salt-free between rides.

## Where the cover helps, and where it does not

A full-length motorcycle cover that reaches the front axle line will keep rain and road spray off the fork legs. That is a real benefit.

A cover that stops at the front wheel spindle leaves the stanchions exposed, which is the common compromise on universal patterns. On a bike with long fork travel, the exposed area is larger.

What no cover does is protect the tubes from impacts while riding. That is what fork guards and, on off-road machines, proper protectors are for. A cover is a storage and parking measure.

## Handlebar and control protection

The handlebar area carries the most expensive small parts on the bike: switchgear, master cylinder, mirrors, and on modern machines a screen and electronics.

A cover helps by keeping rain out of switchgear and off the mirror glass. Two failure modes matter:

**Standing water in switchgear.** Water that enters a switch housing and freezes or corrodes causes intermittent faults that are difficult to diagnose. A cover that sheds water clear of the bar area reduces this.

**Stress at the highest point.** The bar and mirror area is where the cover is most stretched and most wind-loaded. A pattern that pulls tight here will abrade the mirror stalks and stress the top seam. A slightly generous top with a shaped peak performs better than a tight one.

## The lower front of the pattern

This is the part that buyers should scrutinise on a drawing:

- **Does the hem reach the front axle, or stop at the mudguard?**
- **Is there a closure at the front**, so the cover cannot lift and expose the fork legs in wind?
- **Is the front hem reinforced**, since it is dragged over the wheel and mudguard every time?
- **Does the pattern clear the brake calipers and the ABS sensor wiring** without pulling against them?

A cover designed with a front closure and a hem that reaches the axle costs little more to make and protects a considerably more expensive assembly.

## Practical specification

- front hem reaching the front axle line
- front closure to prevent lift
- reinforced front hem
- shaped top with clearance at mirrors and screen
- no strap crossing the fork stanchions
- soft inner face at the mirror stalks
