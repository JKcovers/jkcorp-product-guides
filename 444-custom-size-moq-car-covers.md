# Why Custom Cover Sizes Carry a Different MOQ

A buyer who has agreed a minimum of 300 pieces for a standard cover is often surprised to be quoted 500 for the same cover in a custom size. The gap is not arbitrary and it is not pure margin — it comes from how a cut-and-sew factory actually costs a size.

Understanding the mechanics makes the number negotiable in a way that simply pushing back does not.

## Where the cost sits

**Pattern making.** A new size needs a pattern, and a cover pattern is not a scaled drawing. Panels, mirror pockets, hem allowance and seam positions all change with the body shape. This is a one-off cost, but a real one.

**Nesting and marker efficiency.** Fabric is cut from a marker — a layout of panels on the roll. A standard size shares a marker with the other standard sizes, so the factory can nest efficiently across an order. A custom size usually gets its own marker, and a marker that is not full wastes fabric.

**Cutting setup.** Every size adds a setup, a check and a distinct bundle to track through sewing. The labour per unit rises even when the sewing time does not.

**Testing and approval.** A custom size normally needs its own pre-production sample and fit check on the vehicle.

## Why the MOQ lands where it does

The factory is trying to recover the one-off costs across the run and to justify a marker that will be cut at least a few times. Below a certain quantity, the setup cost per unit becomes larger than the material cost, and the quote either rises sharply or the order is declined.

That threshold is roughly where the marker becomes worth cutting — which is why quoting a custom size at the standard MOQ produces an uncompetitive unit price rather than a refusal.

## Ways to make a custom size viable

**Combine sizes in one order.** A custom size ordered alongside standard sizes can share a container and sometimes part of the marker. Ask whether the custom size can be nested with the rest.

**Commit to a repeat.** A pattern is amortised over repeats. A first order plus a stated second order within a season is a different proposition from a single run, and factories price it differently.

**Accept a longer lead time.** Custom sizes can be slotted into gaps in the cutting schedule. Offering flexibility on timing is often worth more than arguing about quantity.

**Start from the nearest standard size.** If the difference is small, a modified standard pattern is cheaper than a new one. Ask what the actual dimensional delta is before commissioning a new size.

## What to put in writing

- the one-off pattern cost, stated separately from the unit price
- whether the pattern is yours, and what happens if you stop ordering
- the MOQ for the first order and for repeats
- grading rules if you intend to add sizes later

Separating the one-off cost from the unit price is the single most useful step. It turns an opaque higher minimum into two numbers you can evaluate — and it usually reveals that the pattern cost, spread over two or three orders, is smaller than the price premium on a standard size.
