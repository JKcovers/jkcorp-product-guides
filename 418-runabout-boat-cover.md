# Runabout Boat Cover Guide

A runabout is the general-purpose family boat: open cockpit, a windshield, usually a sterndrive or outboard, and a length that fits on a trailer. It is the most common boat in many markets and the one most often bought with a generic cover that does not fit.

The runabout's difficulty is that it is not one shape. The category covers open bow and closed bow, windshield and console, sterndrive and outboard — and each combination changes the cover.

## What the category includes

- **Closed-bow runabouts**, with a deck forward of the windshield.
- **Open-bow runabouts**, which are bowriders in all but name.
- **Sterndrive or inboard** engine layouts, which place the engine under a sun pad.
- **Outboard** versions, which leave the transom open.
- **Small windshields**, usually with a walk-through on open-bow models.

Before specifying, establish which of these the boat is. The pattern differs at the bow, at the transom and at the windshield.

## The bow

**Closed bow.** The foredeck is a solid surface, often curved, which gives the cover a natural high point forward. Drainage is straightforward: the cover slopes aft from the bow.

**Open bow.** The bow is a well, and the cover must be supported over it. This is the same problem as a bowrider, and it is the most common cause of a runabout cover pooling water.

## The windshield

A runabout windshield is usually low, curved and framed, and often has a walk-through section.

- **It should not carry load.** A cover tensioned against the glass or the frame will stress both.
- **The walk-through is a gap.** On an open-bow boat, the cover must bridge it or the pattern must be split there.
- **The frame's finish abrades** at any contact line, so clearance or a soft interface is needed.

## The transom

**Sterndrive.** The engine sits under a sun pad, and the outdrive projects aft and down. The cover's rear hem must clear the outdrive and the trailer, and the sun pad is a good rear support.

**Outboard.** The engine is outside the cover, which means the cover ends at the transom and the engine is unprotected — or the cover extends over it, which changes the pattern considerably.

This single difference alters the rear third of the cover. A pattern sold for "runabouts" without stating which, should be questioned.

## Trailering

Runabouts are overwhelmingly trailered, which sets the minimum specification:

- **A closing hem.** A loose hem at highway speed will lift and tear.
- **No unsupported spans.** The bow-to-windshield span is the usual offender on a closed-bow boat.
- **Strap routing clear of the gelcoat**, with soft interfaces.
- **Rear clearance** at the outdrive, trailer lights and any towed equipment.

## Storage versus mooring

A runabout used on a trailer needs a cover that survives transport and packs down. A runabout kept on a mooring needs one that survives wind and splash, with attachment rather than just a hem.

Both are sold as "runabout covers", and they are different products.

## What to look for

- the bow type stated explicitly
- clearance at the windshield, with no load on glass
- rear clearance matched to sterndrive or outboard
- a closing hem rated for trailering
- support specified for the bow if it is open
- vents, since a runabout cockpit is a large enclosed volume

## Buying notes

Ask for the bow type, the engine type and whether the boat is trailed or moored. Three questions, and they determine almost the whole specification.
