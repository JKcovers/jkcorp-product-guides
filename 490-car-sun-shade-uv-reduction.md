# Car Sun Shade UV Reduction: What Claims Can Be Made

Sun shade packaging is full of numbers. Ninety-nine percent UV blocking, thirty-degree temperature drops, total interior protection. Buyers repeat these figures because competitors do, and the result is a category full of claims that cannot be substantiated when challenged.

This article sets out what a sun shade genuinely does, and how to make claims that survive scrutiny.

## How automotive glass already behaves

Fully tempered automotive glass blocks most ultraviolet B radiation, the shorter wavelength that causes sunburn. It transmits a substantial proportion of ultraviolet A, the longer wavelength associated with material degradation and deeper skin penetration.

This matters commercially. The shade is not the primary UV barrier for the driver and passengers — the glass does much of that. The shade's real contribution is what happens when the vehicle is parked and unoccupied, and how it affects the surfaces inside.

## What the product actually changes

A shade intercepts light before it reaches interior surfaces. That produces three measurable effects:

1. **Lower surface temperatures** on the dashboard, steering wheel and seats, compared with an uncovered vehicle in the same conditions.
2. **Reduced radiant load entering the cabin**, so the air conditioning has less heat to remove when the driver returns.
3. **Less cumulative light exposure** on the materials that degrade — dashboard top, upper door trim, seat bolsters.

These are the effects worth describing. They are mechanisms rather than magic numbers, and they are what the product genuinely delivers.

## Why percentage claims are risky

A UV blocking percentage is only meaningful with a stated measurement method. Which wavelength range was tested? Measured through glass or in open air? By spectrophotometer or by a handheld meter? On the fabric alone or on the assembled product as fitted?

A figure quoted without those parameters cannot be compared with a competitor's figure and cannot be defended if a customer disputes it. In several markets, unsubstantiated performance claims also create exposure under advertising and consumer protection rules.

## How to make a defensible claim

If a supplier wants to publish a performance figure, the claim should carry its test conditions. A properly framed statement names the wavelength range tested, the method, and whether the measurement was taken through glass.

Where a supplier cannot supply test data, the better approach is to describe the mechanism and let the product speak for itself. A shade that is reflective on the outer face, fits the screen properly and stays in place will outperform one with a bigger number printed on the box and a poor fit.

## What buyers should test instead

Rather than chase a UV percentage, evaluate the properties that determine real-world usefulness:

| Property | How to assess |
|---|---|
| Coverage | Does the panel close the gap at the corners of the screen? |
| Reflectivity | Is the outer face genuinely reflective, or merely light-coloured? |
| Dimensional stability | Does it stay flat against the glass after repeated folding? |
| Attachment | Does it stay in place with the visors down and the windows closed? |
| Storage | Does it fold or roll compactly enough to be kept in the vehicle? |

## Positioning advice

Sell comfort and interior preservation, not a lab figure. The customer's experience of a cooler dashboard and a steering wheel that can be gripped is a stronger and more honest argument than a percentage nobody can verify — and it is the claim the product will consistently live up to.
