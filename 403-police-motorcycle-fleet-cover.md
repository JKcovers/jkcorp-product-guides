# Covers for Police Motorcycle Fleets

A police motorcycle fleet buys covers for reasons that have little to do with cosmetics. The machines are expensive, they carry equipment that is sensitive to water, and they are parked in public places where they must remain both protected and conspicuous. Procurement usually runs through a tender or framework agreement, which means the specification has to be written well enough to be compared.

This guide covers what a fleet specification should contain.

## What the cover has to do

**Protect equipment, not just paint.** Police machines carry radios, cameras, emergency lighting, sirens and wiring looms. Water ingress into any of these is a maintenance event, and some of it is a safety issue.

**Keep the machine presentable.** A marked police vehicle is a visible representation of the service. A dirty or wet machine is a presentation problem as well as a maintenance one.

**Support rapid deployment.** An officer returning to the machine at short notice must be able to remove the cover quickly, in the dark, wearing gloves and duty kit.

**Remain conspicuous.** A covered police motorcycle should still read as a police motorcycle, and should be visible to traffic.

**Survive institutional use.** The cover will be fitted by many different officers, stored in a pool, and cleaned in batches.

## Specification points for a tender

**Pattern and coverage.** Full coverage to the ground, with specific clearance for the screen, mirrors, panniers, radio boxes and any rear equipment rack. A generic motorcycle pattern will not clear a police fit-out.

**Equipment clearance.** The cover must not press on aerials, cameras or light bars. Specify a minimum clearance at each fitted item, and require a physical fitment check on the actual machine rather than a representative model.

**Water resistance.** State the requirement in terms of the assembled product, including seams, not a fabric swatch rating. Require evidence of testing on a sewn cover.

**Condensation control.** Equipment stored under a sealed cover in the cold will collect moisture. Specify ventilation, because corrosion in a wiring loom is a far greater cost than a damp cover.

**Fastening.** One-handed operation with gloves, a single identifiable release, and no small loose components that can be dropped or lost on a forecourt.

**Conspicuity.** Retroreflective material positioned at the height of approaching vehicle lamps, plus the fleet's own livery requirements. Confirm the material type by behaviour, not colour.

**Identification.** Marked size or machine class, so covers are matched correctly in a pool.

**Cleaning.** State whether the cover will be machine washed and at what setting, and require the construction to tolerate it.

**Replacement and spares.** A commitment to supply matching replacements for the life of the fleet, including buckles and straps as separate parts.

## Common procurement mistakes

**Buying on unit price.** Fleet covers fail at the hem and the fastenings, and the labour of replacing them is a real cost. Cost per machine per year is the useful metric.

**Skipping the fitment trial.** A cover that fouls a radio box will be cut or left off. Require a trial on at least one fully equipped machine before acceptance.

**Ignoring storage.** Covers stored wet in a pool develop mould and odour. Specify how they will be dried and stored.

## Acceptance testing

A practical acceptance protocol: fit to a fully equipped machine, hose the top seam and hem, inspect the interior, operate the fastening with gloves in low light, and leave it overnight in cool conditions to check condensation.
