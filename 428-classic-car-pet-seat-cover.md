# Pet Cover for Classic Car Interiors

A classic car is the hardest vehicle in which to protect a seat, because almost none of the assumptions built into a modern pet cover hold. There are usually no headrests, the upholstery is original and irreplaceable, the seating may be a single bench, and nothing about the dimensions matches a modern template.

The goal is also different. In a modern car the cover protects resale value; in a classic it protects upholstery that may not be obtainable again.

## What classic interiors demand

**No headrests, or fixed humps.** The standard installation threads straps through removable headrest posts. A classic bench or a low-back bucket seat has nothing to thread through. Anchoring must come from the seatback joint, the seat frame or the base.

**Fragile and irreplaceable material.** Original vinyl, expanded vinyl, wool cloth and leather from the period all react badly to the same two things: abrasion and dye transfer. Both are cover faults rather than dog faults.

**Unusual dimensions.** Bench widths, cushion depths and seatback angles vary enormously between models, and many are outside the range of universal covers.

**A single bench seat, often in front.** Where the front bench carries both driver and dog, the cover has to leave the pedals, column shifter and lap belts clear, and it cannot rely on the shape of modern seat bolsters to hold it.

**Low production volumes.** A vehicle that sold in small numbers has no off-the-shelf cover and usually no pattern, so a custom cut is the realistic option.

## Protecting original upholstery

Two failure modes cause most of the complaints in this situation.

**Abrasion.** A loose cover moves against old vinyl or wool and burnishes the surface. The damage is progressive and cannot be reversed. A cover for a classic must be fitted and tensioned, not draped.

**Dye transfer.** Coloured backings and lower-grade fabrics can transfer colour onto pale or cream upholstery, particularly in a warm interior. On a modern seat this is an annoyance; on original material it is a permanent defect. A soft, undyed or colourfast inner surface is essential, and it is worth requiring written confirmation rather than assuming.

**Hardware contact.** Buckles, hooks and zip pulls should never rest on the seat surface. On a classic, use fabric- or plastic-covered anchors.

## Practical approaches

**A custom-cut flat cover with seatback-joint anchors.** The most reliable answer for a classic bench. It requires measuring the actual seat, including the seatback angle, rather than relying on a nominal model size.

**A shaped cover with a soft, bonded lining.** For low-back bucket seats, a shaped cover holds position without needing headrest posts, provided the lining is soft.

**A throw-over cover used only for transport.** A practical compromise for occasional trips: it can be plain, unlined and undyed, provided it is thick enough not to abrade and is anchored rather than draped.

## Measuring a classic seat

1. Overall bench width at the widest point.
2. Cushion depth from the seatback joint to the front edge.
3. Seatback height, measured to the top of the upholstery.
4. Seatback angle, as a note for the pattern maker.
5. The position of any seatbelts, lap belts and controls that must remain accessible.

## A note on the owner's expectation

Classic owners are the most careful buyers in this category: willing to pay for a correct product, and least tolerant of something that marks the seat. The sensible supplier position is a plain, soft, custom-cut cover with fabric-covered anchors, explicitly offered as non-marking rather than universal.
