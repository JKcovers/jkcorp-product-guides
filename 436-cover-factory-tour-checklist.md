# Cover Factory Tour Checklist

A factory tour is not an audit, and treating it as one is how buyers end up reassured by a visit that proved very little. A tour shows a building and a production line on its best day. An audit tests systems against evidence.

This checklist is for the tour itself — the two hours you actually get — and it is explicit about what a tour cannot tell you.

## Before you arrive

Ask three questions in advance, because the answers are more informative than anything you will see on the day:

- Which lines will be running, and on what?
- Can we see the cutting and sewing areas, not only the finished-goods warehouse?
- Who will be present — sales only, or production and QC?

A supplier who cannot arrange production and QC representation on a scheduled visit is telling you something about how decisions are made internally.

## What to look at on the floor

**Material flow.** Fabric should move in one direction, from inspection and cutting through sewing to packing. If cut panels and finished covers share the same aisle, you are looking at a layout that invites mix-ups.

**Cutting.** Ask whether patterns are nested by hand or by machine. Look at the offcut pile: a large volume of waste suggests either poor nesting or a producer who is not cost-sensitive.

**Sewing stations.** Look at the machines, not the operators' speed. Are seam types consistent across stations? Is there a sample of the current order pinned up as a reference?

**In-process checks.** A line with no measuring station between sewing and packing is relying on the final check to catch everything.

**Packing area.** Count the cartons and compare with the order board. Check whether cartons are labelled with the SKU at the point of packing or afterwards.

## Questions worth asking an operator

Not the manager — the person sewing.

- How do you know this panel is the right size?
- What do you do when a seam looks wrong?
- How many of these do you finish in a shift?

Answers that are vague, or that redirect to a supervisor, usually mean the operator has not been given a standard.

## What a tour cannot prove

A tour does not tell you whether the factory pays wages on time, whether its chemical documentation is current, whether it can hold tolerance across a 5,000-piece order, or whether the line you saw is the line your order will run on. Those need documents, sampling records and an audit.

It also cannot show you the factory under pressure. A calm line in a quiet month looks the same as a well-managed one.

## Using the tour properly

Treat the tour as a filter, not a verdict. It is good at three things: confirming the factory exists at the scale claimed, revealing whether management understands its own process, and giving you the material to ask sharper questions afterwards.

The written follow-up matters more than the visit. Send the specific points you want confirmed — capacity per month, which line your order will run on, who signs the pre-production sample — and see how the answers are handled.
