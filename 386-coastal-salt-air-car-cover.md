# Salt Air Coastal Parking Cover

A car parked near the sea faces a corrosion load that has no inland equivalent. Salt is carried on the wind as fine aerosol, it settles on every exposed surface, and in the presence of moisture it accelerates the corrosion of steel, the pitting of aluminium and the deterioration of paint and brightwork.

Covering the car is the most practical countermeasure. But a coastal cover has a maintenance requirement that most owners underestimate, and getting that wrong makes the cover part of the problem.

## What salt aerosol does

Salt does not need to be visible to be working. The fine fraction deposits continuously and is invisible on a clean car. Its effects follow a clear pattern:

- **Moisture reactivates it.** Salt is hygroscopic and draws water from the air. A car that is merely damp behaves like a car that has been splashed with seawater.
- **It attacks where coatings are thinnest.** Brake discs and calipers, wheel fasteners, fasteners and brackets under the bonnet, and any stone chip through the paint.
- **It reaches places rain never does.** The fine aerosol fraction penetrates panel gaps, mirror housings, door drains and the underside of the body.

The most common coastal casualty is not the paint — it is the brake discs, which can flash a rust film within days of parking near the shore.

## What a cover actually achieves

A cover removes the continuous deposition. Salt in the air still circulates, but it lands on a removable panel instead of on paint, glass and brake surfaces. That alone changes the maintenance equation: the car stays cleaner, and wash intervals stretch from every few days to every few weeks.

Cover requirements in this setting are specific:

- **Full coverage.** Salt settles everywhere. Partial covers leave the horizontal surfaces, which receive the highest deposition, exposed.
- **Fine-particle blocking.** The relevant property is the finest particle the fabric stops. A tight weave outperforms a heavy open weave against aerosol.
- **Secure fit.** Flapping lifts the hem and lets salt-laden air circulate underneath, where the trapped salt then abrades the paint.
- **A surface that can be rinsed repeatedly.** The cover will need washing far more often than an inland cover, and it must survive that.

## The coastal maintenance rule

**A dirty cover is worse than no cover.** Salt accumulates in the fabric, and the next time the cover is fitted it presses that salt against the paint, with the trapped dust acting as an abrasive. Coastal covers therefore need rinsing on a regular schedule, not on a seasonal one.

The practical routine:

1. Rinse the cover at least weekly, and after any onshore wind event.
2. Always rinse the car before refitting the cover, and never fit a cover over a salt film.
3. Dry the cover completely before folding. A cover stored damp with salt in it corrodes itself as well as the next car it touches.
4. Rinse the car's underside, brake area and wheel arches periodically, because no cover covers everything.
5. Treat exposed fasteners and brightwork with a protective wax or sealant.

## Buying for a coastal vehicle

- full coverage with a tight, elasticated hem
- fine-particle blocking outer fabric
- a material that tolerates frequent rinsing
- a light colour, which reduces the amount of heat the cover retains while parked in strong coastal sun
