# Temperature Swings and Cover Condensation

The most counter-intuitive problem in cover use is a car that is bone dry on the outside of the cover and wet underneath it. It happens when the temperature swings, and it is physics rather than a product defect.

## The mechanism, step by step

A cover follows the temperature of the sky it faces. On a clear evening the fabric radiates heat away and cools faster than the surrounding air. Three things then happen in sequence:

1. The cover's outer surface drops below the dew point, and moisture from the air condenses on it. That is ordinary outside dew, and it is harmless.
2. Because the cover is now cold, it also cools the air trapped in the space between the fabric and the bodywork.
3. Air that cools cannot hold as much vapour. The vapour that was in that trapped air condenses — on the bodywork, on the glass, and on the inside of the cover itself.

The result is under-cover condensation: the car is wet under a dry cover, and the water is on the surfaces the cover was meant to protect.

## Why some covers do this and others do not

The difference is what happens to vapour after it condenses.

**A breathable cover lets vapour pass out.** Moisture that reaches the inner surface can migrate through the fabric and escape as the day warms. The car dries under the cover, following the same cycle it would follow in the open.

**A fully waterproof, non-breathable cover traps it.** Vapour cannot leave, so the trapped air stays saturated and the surfaces stay wet for as long as the cover is on. This is the situation that produces water spotting, mould in seams and trim, and corrosion on brightwork.

That is why the heaviest, most heavily waterproofed cover is not automatically the best one. Where daily temperature swings are large, breathability matters more than maximum water resistance.

## The other variable: air volume and fit

A loose cover traps a larger volume of air, and a larger volume holds more vapour to manage. A close-fitting cover leaves a thinner air layer, which equilibrates with outside air faster and condenses less. Fit matters for moisture as well as for wind: a baggy cover behaves like a small greenhouse.

## Seasonal patterns

- **Spring and autumn.** The largest daily swings in most temperate regions, and therefore the worst under-cover condensation.
- **Winter.** Absolute humidity is lower, so condensation is less, but freeze-thaw cycling brings its own problems — moisture that condenses and then freezes can bond the cover to the panel.
- **Summer.** Small swings and low humidity at night, so condensation is rarely an issue.
- **Coastal and lakeside locations.** Higher humidity makes every cycle worse.

## Practical countermeasures

1. **Specify a breathable coated or laminated fabric** rather than a solid waterproof film.
2. **Fit the cover accurately.** Less trapped air means less trapped vapour.
3. **Do not fit the cover wet.** Allowing a vehicle to dry first removes the moisture source.
4. **Ventilate the storage space** if the car is kept indoors; the cover cannot fix a saturated room.
5. **Air the cover periodically.** Removing it on a dry day lets both the cover and the car dry fully.

## The specification conclusion

Where the temperature crosses the dew point regularly, the right cover is breathable and well-fitted, with water resistance sufficient for rain but not so absolute that vapour is trapped. That combination manages both directions of the moisture problem.
