# Building an OEM Cover Catalog That Sells

A cover catalog is not a price list with photographs. For a brand buying OEM, it is the document that decides whether a distributor can order without asking questions — and most catalogs fail at exactly that point, because they describe the product rather than specifying it.

This guide covers the structure that works.

## Start with the SKU logic, not the layout

Before any design work, decide how a product is identified. A usable code carries four things:

- product family (indoor stretch, outdoor, van, pet)
- size or fitment
- colour
- packaging variant

If the code cannot be read aloud on a phone call and understood, it will be mis-ordered. Two letters and four digits is usually enough; a 20-character code with embedded dates is not.

## One page per SKU

Each SKU needs a page a buyer can act on without calling:

**The specification block.** Fabric, coating, weight, construction, hem, mirror pockets, closures, and the vehicle range the size covers. Numbers, not adjectives.

**The fitment table.** Which vehicles each size covers, stated as a range rather than a list of every model. A list of 200 models is impressive once and unusable afterwards.

**Dimensions.** Flat dimensions and, for stretch products, the elongation they are designed for. This is what allows a distributor to answer a customer's question without escalating.

**Packaging and carton data.** Units per carton, carton dimensions, gross weight, and cartons per container. Without these, the buyer cannot quote freight, and a quote without freight is not a quote.

**Compliance status.** Which test reports exist and what they cover. State the standard, not the word "certified".

## Photography that does the work

Three images per product, minimum:

- the cover fitted, in a setting that shows scale
- a detail shot of the construction — seam, hem, mirror pocket
- a packaging shot, if packaging is part of the offer

Shoot the fit rather than the fabric. A buyer cannot judge fabric from a photograph, but they can judge whether the cover looks tailored or baggy, which is the decision they are actually making.

## What to leave out

- superlatives about quality
- unverifiable performance claims
- a page of company history at the front
- prices in the main catalog if you sell through distributors with different terms

The catalog's job is to make ordering unambiguous. Every page that does not do that is a page someone skips.

## Keeping it current

A catalog date-stamped on the cover, with a revision number, prevents the most common support problem: a distributor ordering from a two-year-old PDF.

Keep one master file, version it, and tell distributors which version is current. If sizes or fabrics change, the version changes.
