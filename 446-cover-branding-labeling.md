# Cover Branding and Labeling Options

Branding a cover involves four separate decisions that are often treated as one: how the logo goes on the fabric, what label goes inside, what tag goes outside, and what information the product is legally required to carry.

Getting the first three right and the fourth wrong is common, and the fourth is the one that causes problems at customs and in retail.

## Branding the fabric

**Woven label, sewn on.** Durable and premium-feeling, and it survives washing and sun. Adds a sewing operation and a label cost per unit.

**Printed directly on the cover.** Screen print is inexpensive and holds up on flat areas but cracks over a seam or a stretch zone. Heat transfer gives finer detail and better elongation, at a higher cost.

**Embroidery.** Uncommon on covers because the needle perforates a coated fabric, creating a path for water. Where it is used, it belongs on an uncoated panel.

**Rubber or silicone patch.** Reads as technical and suits outdoor ranges. Needs a heat press or adhesive that tolerates the coating.

For a stretch cover, the constraint is elongation: a print that cannot move with the fabric will craze at the edges within a season. Ask for the elongation of the decoration, not just the fabric.

## Labels inside the product

At minimum:

- fibre composition, using the defined fibre names for the market
- country of origin
- care instructions that match what the fabric can survive
- the brand and, where required, an importer or responsible operator address

The care instruction is the one most often copied from another product. A coated cover that is machine washed at the temperature printed on a generic label will delaminate, and the label then becomes evidence against you.

## Tags and retail presentation

Hang tags carry the selling story: size, vehicle range, key features. They are also the cheapest place to correct a customer's expectation — a tag that says "indoor use" prevents a return that a photograph could not.

Barcodes belong on the tag or the polybag, not only on the carton. A retailer receiving a carton of unlabelled units cannot book them into stock.

## The compliance layer

For most markets the labelling obligation is not optional and not brand-dependent. Fibre composition and origin are typical requirements; the exact wording and language depend on the destination.

This is worth confirming per market rather than assuming, because a label that satisfies one retailer's spec sheet may not satisfy the regulation behind it.

## Practical sequencing

1. Fix the fabric and construction first — decoration choices depend on them.
2. Choose the decoration method against the stretch and the wash life.
3. Draft the care label from the actual material, not from a template.
4. Confirm the mandatory content for each destination market.
5. Only then design the tag and packaging.

Doing it in that order avoids the usual outcome: a beautiful tag printed before anyone checked whether the cover can legally be sold with those care instructions.
