# Boat Cover Tie-Downs — How Many Points and Where

A boat cover is only as secure as its tie-downs. The wrong number or placement of tie-downs lets the cover lift, flap, and fail at the worst time. Here's the correct tie-down strategy.

## Why Tie-Downs Matter

Boat covers catch wind like sails. A 20-foot cover at 40 mph wind generates hundreds of pounds of lift force. Without enough secure points, the cover lifts, fills with air, and either tears or abrades the hull finish.

## The General Rule

The industry guideline: one tie-down point for every 2 feet of boat length, minimum.

| Boat Length | Minimum Tie-Downs |
|:--:|:--:|
| 16 ft | 8 |
| 20 ft | 10 |
| 24 ft | 12 |
| 30 ft | 15 |

## Where to Place Them

**Bow:** Two tie-downs crossed over the bow — this is the highest-lift area
**Gunwale line:** Tie-downs every 2-3 feet along both sides, alternating
**Stern:** Two reinforced points at the rear corners
**Windshield/T-top:** Extra straps over the tallest structures

## Tie-Down Types

| Type | Best For |
|------|----------|
| Ratchet straps | Maximum tension, long-term storage |
| Shock cords (bungee) | Quick on/off, light use |
| Webbing with cam buckles | Balanced — secure but fast |
| Rope | Emergency use only |

## Common Mistakes

- **Too few points** — the cover stretches between tie-downs
- **Tying to fragile parts** — cleats and rails bend; use trailer frame or dedicated rings
- **Uneven tension** — tighter on one side causes the cover to shift
- **No bow support** — the bow needs both cross-tie-downs AND a center pole

## The Bow Problem

The bow is the hardest area to secure — it's pointed, high, and catches wind first. A center support pole plus crossed tie-downs keeps the bow tight.
