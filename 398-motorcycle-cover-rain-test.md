# Motorcycle Cover Rain Test Standards

"Waterproof" is the most abused word in the cover trade, because several different tests all legitimately produce a waterproof-sounding result. A supplier can quote a number that is real, measured, and irrelevant to how a cover behaves in a storm.

For buyers, the useful skill is not memorising limits but knowing which test answers which question.

## The three tests that get confused

**Spray rating.** A specimen is subjected to a directed water spray and assessed visually for surface wetting. It answers: does water bead and run off the face fabric? It does not answer whether water passes through the fabric under pressure, and it says nothing about seams.

**Hydrostatic head.** A column of water is applied to one side and the pressure at which it penetrates is recorded. It answers: how much pressure can the fabric resist? It is a good measure of the base cloth and coating, and it is the number most often quoted.

**Simulated rain / shower testing.** A garment or cover is exposed to falling water, often on a moving form, and the interior inspected. It answers: does the assembled product leak? This is the only one of the three that tests seams, closures and the pattern as a whole.

A cover can have an excellent hydrostatic head and still leak at the seams, which is why the third test matters most and is quoted least.

## Why motorcycle covers are harder than flat fabric

A motorcycle cover is not a flat panel. It is a shaped assembly with:

- **seams at the highest and most exposed points**, where the pattern curves over bars and screen
- **stitch holes** that create a line of capillary paths if unsealed
- **closures and hems** where water can pool
- **a large unsupported span** between bars and front wheel that can hold water in a sag

Testing flat fabric says nothing about any of the above.

## What to ask for

A credible response to a rain question includes:

1. **Which test** was run, described precisely rather than named.
2. **Whether the test was on fabric or on a finished cover.**
3. **The seam treatment** — taped, welded, sealed or none — and whether the seams were included in the test.
4. **Whether the test was static or under airflow**, because a cover in wind sheds water differently from one hanging still.
5. **A sample you can test yourself.** This remains the most reliable evidence available.

## A practical buyer's test

You do not need a laboratory:

- Fit the cover to the machine.
- Direct a hose at the highest seam — over the bars and screen — for a sustained period.
- Inspect the inside of that seam immediately, and again after the cover has sat wet for a while.
- Repeat at the lowest hem, where splash accumulates.
- Check whether water pools in any sag, and whether that pool drains or sits.

A cover that passes the top seam and hem will handle ordinary rain. A cover that fails there will fail in service regardless of the number on the spec sheet.

## Common specification traps

**Quoting a fabric rating as a product rating.** The most frequent substitution.

**Testing a swatch rather than a sewn cover.** Seams are where leaks begin.

**Ignoring the underside.** Water splashing up from the road is often a greater load on a low-slung cover than rain falling on it.

**Treating "water-resistant" and "waterproof" as interchangeable.** They describe different levels of resistance, and a cover sold as waterproof should be able to withstand sustained water, not a shower.
