# Bowrider Boat Cover Guide

A bowrider is the most common family boat shape and one of the hardest to cover well. The reason is the open bow: a large, open well at the front of the boat, forward of the windshield, with seating and often a filler cushion. Water that lands on the bow has nowhere to run except into that well.

A cover that simply drapes over a bowrider will pool water in the bow and, within a season, stretch, leak and stain the seating beneath it.

## What makes the shape difficult

- **The open bow is a basin.** Unlike a deck boat or a cuddy, there is no raised structure to shed water forward.
- **The walk-through windshield** creates a gap in the middle of the boat where the cover must either bridge or be split.
- **The bow rises**, so the cover runs uphill from the cockpit to the bow — which is the opposite of what drainage needs.
- **Snap-in carpet and cushions** are usually present and are the first things damaged by standing water.
- **No hardtop or tower** on most bowriders, so there is nothing to support the cover in the middle.

The design problem is therefore support and drainage, not coverage.

## Support: the bow needs help

Because the cover runs uphill toward the bow, it needs to be lifted so water sheds aft or to the sides.

Practical options:

- **A bow pole**, set on the bow floor, lifting the cover into a peak that drains aft.
- **A support frame** across the bow opening, resting on the seat bases.
- **A self-supporting pattern** that is cut to bridge the bow with a raised ridge, which only works if the fabric is stiff enough and the span is short.

Without one of these, the cover becomes a bowl. This is the most common failure in bowrider covers, and a design decision rather than a fabric problem.

## Drainage and the windshield gap

The walk-through section is a structural gap. Two approaches work:

**A single cover with a reinforced bridge.** The fabric spans the walk-through, supported by the windshield frame on both sides. Simple, but the bridge is a high-wear area and must be reinforced.

**A two-part cover**, with the bow section and cockpit section joined by a zipped or strapped connection. More complex, but each section drains independently.

Whichever is used, the cover should not rely on the windshield glass to carry load. Glass is not a structural support, and a cover tensioned against it will eventually crack or displace the frame.

## Trailering versus mooring

A bowrider is usually trailered, and that changes the requirement:

- **Trailering** loads the cover at highway speed, so the hem must close and the bow must be supported by a fixed pole, not a loose one.
- **Mooring** is a static load, so a slightly looser fit with good drainage is acceptable, and ventilation matters more.

A cover sold for both uses must be specified for the harder one.

## What to look for

- a bow support pole or frame included or specified
- a reinforced bridge at the walk-through
- a closed, elasticated or drawstring hem
- vents to control condensation over the seating
- a pattern that clears the windshield frame without loading it
- straps that do not cross the glass

## Buying notes

Ask whether the cover is designed for a bowrider specifically, and whether a bow pole is required. A generic "open boat" cover will fit the length and fail at the bow, which is exactly where a bowrider differs from every other shape.
