# Dirt Bike and Enduro Cover Guide

A dirt bike is the least cover-friendly motorcycle shape there is. It has no fairing to drape over, a high front fender that sits well above the tyre, sharp number plates, exposed forks and a seat that is narrow and tall. A cover patterned for a road bike will bridge between the fender and the bars, collect water in the gap, and flap itself apart.

Covering an enduro or motocross bike is a separate design problem.

## Why the shape fights a standard cover

- **The high front fender** creates a shelf. Fabric stretched from the bars to the front axle sags onto the fender and holds water there.
- **No bodywork** means there is nothing for the cover to rest on, so it hangs in catenary curves between the bars, seat and rear fender.
- **Sharp edges** — number plates, footpegs, brake pedal, sprocket — abrade a cover from the inside.
- **Tall, narrow profile** makes the cover tall and slim, which is exactly the shape that catches wind.
- **Knobby tyres** and a high ground clearance leave a large open volume under the bike that the cover must either bridge or close.

A dirt bike cover is therefore either a close, shaped pattern or a loose bag — and the loose bag is the one that flaps.

## Two different jobs

It is worth separating the two situations, because they need different products.

**Home storage.** The bike sits for days or weeks between rides. The priorities are dust, rain, UV and condensation. A shaped cover with a drawstring hem and a vent works well, and the bike is stationary so wind loading is manageable.

**Transport and paddock use.** The bike is on a trailer, a hitch carrier or in a van, or it is standing in a paddock overnight at an event. Here the priorities are completely different: the cover must survive highway-speed airflow, straps must not chafe the paint, and it must come off quickly when the bike is needed.

A cover marketed as "waterproof" is not necessarily rated for transport. Highway airflow imposes loads no static fitment produces, and a loose cover at speed will balloon and tear.

## What to specify

**A shaped top.** A pattern that follows the bar-to-seat-to-rear-fender line rather than a rectangle. Even a simple taper reduces pooling on the front fender.

**A closed or elasticated hem.** A drawstring at the bottom keeps the cover from inflating. This is the single most effective anti-flap measure.

**Internal protection at sharp points.** Reinforced panels or a soft lining at the number plates and footpegs.

**Vents.** A dirt bike cover that cannot breathe will condense water onto the bike every cold night. Vents at the top let vapour escape.

**Transport straps, if the cover will be used on a trailer.** Separate, load-bearing straps rather than the fitting hem.

## Cleaning after a muddy ride

Mud is the normal condition, not the exception. Hose the cover while it is still damp, work the mud out of the weave with a soft brush, and dry it fully before folding. A cover folded wet with mud in the seams will smell and will lose its coating faster.

Do not pressure-wash a coated cover at close range. It drives water into the seams and strips the coating at the stitching.

## Buying notes

- shaped for a high-fender bike, not a road-bike pattern
- elasticated or drawstring hem
- reinforced panels at plates and pegs
- vents at the top
- separate transport straps if used on a trailer
- easily hosed and quick to dry
