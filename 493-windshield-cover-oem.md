# Windshield Cover OEM and Private Label Guide

Windscreen covers are one of the more approachable products to take to a private label programme. Construction is simple — a panel, a lining, an edge treatment and a retention system — and there is no tooling barrier for a textile product. The difficulty lies in getting four or five specification decisions right before the first production run, because each one is expensive to change later.

## What a supplier needs before quoting

A quotation is only comparable with another supplier's if the brief is complete. For this product the brief reduces to five points:

1. **Fit strategy** — universal panel, a small number of universal sizes, or vehicle-specific patterns.
2. **Dimensions** — base width, top width, centre height and corner radius for each size in the range.
3. **Materials** — outer fabric and weight, inner lining, and the water-resistance requirement for the outer face.
4. **Retention system** — magnetic edges, mirror straps, door-shut flaps, elastic hem, or a combination; plus reinforcement details at each attachment point.
5. **Branding** — plain, printed outer face, or a sewn-in label; artwork, placement, and whether the lining carries a label too.

A brief that omits any of these will produce a price that cannot be compared and a sample that may pass or fail for reasons nobody can identify.

## Choosing a fit strategy for a private label

This is the highest-leverage decision, because it determines the size of the inventory commitment.

A single universal size keeps SKU count and cost down, but fits poorly on large or unusually shaped screens. A small range — a compact, a mid-size and a large — covers most vehicles with acceptable fit and is what most private label programmes settle on. Vehicle-specific patterns give the best fit and price point, but require accurate measurement work and a much larger commitment.

## Retention and reinforcement

Cheap covers fail at the attachment points. On a magnetic design, that means magnet quality and encapsulation. On a strap design, it means stitching and the strength of the strap itself. On a door-shut flap design, it means the durability of the flap where the door closes on it.

Specify reinforcement explicitly: bar-tacks or box stitching at every load-bearing point, and a doubled layer of fabric under each magnet. These are inexpensive additions that determine whether the product survives a season.

## Materials and print

A reflective, water-resistant outer face with a soft inner lining serves both winter and summer use, which extends the selling season and supports the price. The lining is the product's most important safety property on glass, so specify it verifiably — a supplier should be able to state the material without marking glass.

For printed programmes, keep artwork clear of the edges and any fold lines. Print across a fold cracks before the base fabric does, and the failure is highly visible.

## Sampling and quality control

Sample against a real vehicle, not a bench measurement. Fit the sample to the largest screen in the intended range, test retention in wind, and check that the cover comes away cleanly after a freeze.

Production checks worth requiring:
- Dimensional consistency against the approved sample
- Magnet strength and encapsulation, where applicable
- Stitch integrity at every attachment point
- Lining material consistency between lots
- Print adhesion and colour consistency between runs

## Packaging and freight

A cover that folds compactly ships efficiently, which materially affects landed cost. Confirm the folded dimensions and packaging format early, and specify a storage sleeve for retail — it improves presentation and reduces creasing.
