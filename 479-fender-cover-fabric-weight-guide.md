# Fender Cover Fabric and Weight Guide

Fabric choice decides how a fender cover behaves in use: whether it lies flat, whether it absorbs oil, whether a dropped spanner leaves a dent in the paint beneath it, and how long it lasts. Catalogues often reduce this to a weight figure, which tells only part of the story.

## What the weight figure means

Fabric weight describes the mass of material per unit area. A heavier fabric is generally denser, stiffer and more resistant to puncture, and it holds its shape better on a curved wing.

Weight alone, however, says nothing about fibre type, coating, backing, or construction at the edges. Two covers of identical weight can perform very differently if one has a soft fleece backing and the other has an abrasive reverse face.

## The layers that matter

A fender cover is a sandwich, and each layer has a job:

| Layer | Function | Failure mode if wrong |
|---|---|---|
| Face | Resists oil, solvents and abrasion | Absorbs fluid, stains, transfers contamination |
| Core or padding | Absorbs impact from tools and components | Dropped tools mark the paint |
| Backing | Protects paint from the cover itself | The cover scratches the surface it protects |
| Edge binding | Holds the layers and the magnets together | Fraying; magnets escape the seam |

The backing is the most important layer for paint protection and the one most often skipped in budget products. A soft, non-abrasive backing is not a premium extra — it is the product's core safety property.

## Face materials

**Vinyl and PVC-coated fabrics.** Wipe clean, resistant to oil and most workshop fluids, and durable. The practical choice for professional use, where contamination between vehicles is a real concern.

**Coated textiles.** Lighter, softer and easier to fold, with good fluid resistance from the coating. Suitable for retail and light service use.

**Heavy untreated textile.** Comfortable to lean on and good at absorbing impact, but it holds contamination and needs a proper wash regime rather than a wipe.

## Padding and impact

Technicians set tools down on the cover. Padding determines whether that impact is absorbed or transferred. A thin cover with no core will transmit the shock straight through to the panel, which defeats part of the purpose.

For professional use, a modest padded core is worth the additional weight. For retail, padding is also a comfort feature and sells on feel.

## How weight affects handling

A heavier cover drapes less, lies flatter and stays put more readily — useful on a curved wing where a light cover tends to lift at the edges. The trade-off is handling: a heavy cover is harder to position and to fold for storage, and it costs more to ship.

The practical balance for most workshops is a mid-weight cover with a wipe-clean face and a soft backing. Heavier constructions suit large vehicles and heavy equipment, where a bigger panel needs more mass to stay flat.

## What to specify and verify

Specify fibre type, weight per unit area, coating, backing material and edge construction — not simply a weight. Then verify the four points that determine field performance: fluid resistance on the face, softness of the backing, stitch integrity at the edges and around each magnet, and whether the cover lies flat on a real wing with a technician leaning on it.
