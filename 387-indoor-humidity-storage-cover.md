# Extreme Humidity Indoor Storage Cover

Moving a car indoors feels like the obvious answer to a wet climate. In practice, an enclosed garage or shed in a humid region can be a worse environment than an open carport, because indoors there is no wind to carry moisture away and no sun to dry the surfaces.

The result is a well-known paradox in vehicle storage: the car that is kept inside is the one that develops mould in the trim, corrosion on the brightwork and a damp, musty interior.

## Why enclosed storage can be worse

Moisture gets into a garage from several directions, and enclosed spaces hold it:

- **Ground moisture.** Concrete slabs wick water from the soil below and release it as vapour into the space, particularly after rain.
- **Temperature swings.** A garage that warms during the day and cools at night cycles its air across the dew point, and the moisture condenses on the coldest surfaces — which are usually the car's panels and glass.
- **Poor ventilation.** An enclosed garage trades air slowly. Vapour produced inside stays inside.
- **A wet car.** Putting a wet vehicle into a closed garage releases a large volume of water into the space over the following hours.

The damage this produces is a specific list: mould on fabric and leather, corrosion on fasteners and brightwork, a musty smell that does not clear with airing, and water spotting on paint and glass.

## What a cover should do indoors

The instinct is to specify a waterproof cover for a damp environment. That instinct is wrong for indoor storage.

A waterproof cover traps moisture underneath it. If the space is humid and the cover does not breathe, the air beneath the cover reaches saturation and stays there, which is the worst available condition for the paint and trim.

An indoor storage cover should be:

- **breathable**, so vapour that reaches it can pass through
- **soft on the inner surface**, because it is handled and refitted often and the car has presumably been cleaned
- **dust-blocking**, since the main everyday threat indoors is dust rather than weather
- **light or light-coloured**, so it does not create a warm, damp microclimate

Waterproofing is a low priority indoors. Breathability is the whole point.

## Managing the space, not just the car

The cover handles dust and direct contact. The moisture problem is solved by the environment:

1. **Ventilate.** Cross-ventilation beats sealing. A garage with two open paths for air moves vapour out; a sealed one accumulates it.
2. **Dehumidify in the worst months.** A dehumidifier, or a simple moisture trap, changes the physics more than any cover can.
3. **Keep the floor dry.** A slab that stays wet is a permanent vapour source. Addressing drainage usually outperforms any interior measure.
4. **Never store a wet car.** Dry the vehicle before putting it away, including the door shuts and the underside.
5. **Lift the car off a damp floor** if the storage period is long, so air can move underneath.

## A note on the opposite mistake

Some owners store a car in a warm, dry garage under a tight, waterproof cover, and then wonder why the glass is wet on the inside in the morning. That is not a cover failure. It is condensation forming on the coldest surface in the room, and the fix is breathability and ventilation, not a heavier cover.

## Buying notes

- breathable fabric first, waterproofing second
- soft inner surface for frequent handling
- dust-blocking weave
- a fit that allows the cover to lie without stress on the panel edges
