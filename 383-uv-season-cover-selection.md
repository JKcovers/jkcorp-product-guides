# UV Season Cover Selection

UV exposure is cumulative. A car does not need a specific "UV event" to sustain UV damage — it accumulates every clear day, and the seasons that matter most are the ones that feel least threatening: long, bright spring and early-summer days with moderate temperatures.

Selecting a cover for UV is therefore a question of matching the cover to the exposure a vehicle actually sees, not to a marketing number.

## What drives UV load

UV intensity at a given location depends on several factors, and they compound:

- **Latitude and altitude.** Both increase the intensity of UV reaching the ground. A vehicle at altitude can see substantially more UV than the same vehicle at sea level at the same latitude.
- **Season and sun angle.** Spring and summer carry the highest daily totals, but a clear winter day at altitude still delivers meaningful exposure.
- **Time of day.** The middle of the day carries the greatest intensity.
- **Reflected UV.** Snow, sand, water and light-coloured ground reflect UV upwards onto the sides and underside of a vehicle — which is why boats and cars near snow or water weather differently.
- **Exposure hours.** A car parked outdoors all day accumulates far more than one parked in a garage and driven occasionally.

The specification should follow the vehicle's actual parking pattern.

## What a cover changes

A cover works by intercepting UV before it reaches paint, plastic and rubber. That is straightforward. What varies between products is:

- **How much of the surface is covered.** A full cover blocks UV everywhere. A windscreen-only product blocks one area and leaves the roof, bonnet and boot exposed — and those horizontal surfaces receive the highest dose.
- **How the fabric behaves over time.** A fabric that degrades in sunlight protects well in year one and poorly in year three. UV stabilisation and the base polymer decide the service life.
- **Whether the cover traps heat.** Blocking UV and trapping heat are different properties. A cover can be effective against UV and still raise the temperature under it, which is a separate problem with separate answers.

That third point is the one buyers conflate most often. UV protection and heat reduction are not the same claim, and a product should be specified for whichever of the two the customer actually needs.

## Matching the cover to the season

**Spring and early summer.** The highest cumulative UV in most temperate regions, often accompanied by pollen and tree debris. Light-coloured fabric reflects rather than absorbs, which suits this period.

**High summer.** UV is high and heat becomes the dominant concern alongside it. Reflective or light-coloured outer surfaces are the practical direction.

**Autumn.** UV declines, but wet debris and condensation take over as the main threats. UV performance matters less than water handling in this period.

**Winter and snow.** Direct UV is lower at low altitude, but snow reflection adds load from below and sideways, and freeze-thaw cycling is the dominant damage mechanism. Coverage matters more than UV specification here.

## What to ask a supplier

Percentage UV claims are difficult to defend without a stated test method, the wavelength band covered and the fabric condition tested. The defensible questions are different:

- what polymer and UV stabilisation system the outer fabric uses
- whether the fabric has been tested after weathering, not just when new
- how the cover behaves in the specific exposure the customer faces
- whether the product claims UV protection, heat reduction, or both

A supplier who can separate those claims is usually a supplier who understands the product.
