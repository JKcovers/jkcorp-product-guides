# Building an After-Sales Plan for Cover Distribution

Warranty is the promise. After-sales is the operation that keeps it — and for a distributor, the operation is what determines whether the brand is worth carrying for a second season.

Most after-sales problems in this category are not quality problems. They are information problems: a customer reports a problem, the distributor cannot identify the batch, the supplier cannot reproduce the issue, and a small claim becomes a slow argument.

## Start with traceability

Every carton should carry enough information to answer four questions without opening anything:

- which order it came from
- which production run
- which size and colour
- when it was packed

A batch code on the carton and on the product label is the minimum. Without it, a defect report describes a product that cannot be located, and the only available response is a blanket replacement.

## Define the response path

A workable structure has three tiers:

**Tier one — information.** The distributor answers questions about fit, care and installation from the catalog and spec sheets. Most contacts end here, and they cost nothing if the documents are good.

**Tier two — replacement or credit.** A confirmed defect is resolved without returning the goods, for low-value items where return freight exceeds the product value. Set a value threshold in advance.

**Tier three — investigation.** A pattern of failures, or a claim above the threshold, triggers a batch review with photographs and retained samples.

Publishing the tiers internally stops every complaint from escalating to the supplier by default.

## Spare parts and consumables

Covers have few serviceable parts, but some matter: replacement straps, storage bags, repair patches for small tears. A distributor who can send a strap resolves a complaint that would otherwise become a return.

Decide which of these you stock, and at what ratio. A small allowance per container is usually enough.

## Records that make the next order better

Collect three data points from every claim: the batch, the failure mode, and the use condition. After a season, those three columns show which failure is a supplier issue, which is a specification issue, and which is a customer-expectation issue.

That distinction is the difference between reordering the same product and reordering a better one.

## What to agree with the supplier

- a named contact for claims, and a response time
- the evidence required for a claim to be accepted
- whether credit or replacement is the default
- how a batch-level issue is handled
- who owns the cost of return freight at each tier

Agreeing these before the first container is straightforward. Agreeing them while a customer is waiting is not.
